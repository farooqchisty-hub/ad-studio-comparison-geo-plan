How to clone winning Meta ads without copying them.

Keep the winning ad's structure (hook, angle, proof, offer, format, length) and rebuild each part with your own product and proof.

Last checked 6 Nov 2026·By [Pending: author name]·Disclosure: we make Brevo Ads

The method in 5 frames

How do you clone a winning ad?

  1. Spot a proven winner.

    Search the Meta Ad Library and save links to ads live 90+ days.[1][3]

  2. Break down its structure.

    One line per part, hook to length, in your own words.

  3. Swap in your product.

    Keep the structure and refill each slot with your product and proof.

  4. Make 3 distinct variants.

    Meta treats look-alikes as one creative. Vary the format, angle or buyer.[4]

  5. Test, then log the winner.

    Pick the metric first, usually cost per purchase, then log the winning concept.

FAQ

What else do people ask about winning ads?

What is a winning ad?

A winning ad is one a brand keeps paying to run because it sells. From outside the brand, runtime is the best public signal. Look for ads still live after about 90 days, ideally in several versions. Runtime suggests a winner but can't prove profit.

Is it legal to copy a competitor's ad?

Copying the structure is usually fine; copying the ad usually isn't. Copyright leaves ideas, angles and formats free, but their footage, images, music, logo, exact words and reviews stay protected. This is general information, so ask a lawyer about a specific ad.

How do you find winning Facebook ads?

Search Meta's free Ad Library by brand or product keyword and check each card's "Started running on" date. Shortlist ads live 90 days or more, especially ones with several versions. Ad Radar in Brevo Ads ranks ads by days running.

Where can I see competitor ads?

Use free public ad libraries. The Meta Ad Library covers Facebook and Instagram ads, and Google's Ads Transparency Center covers Google ads. Search by brand name or keyword, then note how long each ad has been running.

What is an ad swipe file?

An ad swipe file is a saved collection of ads you study for ideas. For cloning, keep each ad's public Ad Library link and your six-line breakdown, from hook to length. You don't need to download their creative.

It's a bet, though. Runtime is a proxy, and some ads keep running because nobody turned them off. Look for a second signal.

The card may also say "This ad has multiple versions." That often means the brand is putting more creative behind that concept.

And pick competitors who sell to your customer at a similar price. A winning ad for a nine-dollar lip balm tells you little about a hundred-and-twenty-dollar serum.

01:38

When you find one, save the Ad Library link. Don't download the creative. You need the link to come back to it, and you won't be reusing the file.

01:55Break down its structure

01:55

Step two: break the ad into six parts and write each one down in your own words.

02:03

One, the hook. The first line of text, or the first three seconds of video. What stops the scroll?

02:12

Two, the angle. The argument for buying. A problem, a desire, a comparison, or a way of seeing yourself.

02:22

Three, the proof. What makes you believe it? A demo, a texture close-up, a review count, a before and after, an expert.

02:33

Four, the offer. Full price, a percentage off, a bundle, free shipping or a guarantee.

02:41

Five, the format. Static, carousel, talking-head UGC, a product demo, a meme, or something that looks like a notes app.

02:52

Six, the length. Seconds for video, word count for copy, and the aspect ratio.

03:00

Write one line per part. If you can describe the ad in six lines without quoting it, you've separated the structure from the expression. That's the part you can reuse. The worksheet on the page has these six rows ready to copy.

03:20Swap in your product

03:20

Step three: keep the structure and swap in everything that's yours.

03:25

Say the winner is a static ad for a tinted sunscreen. The hook calls out makeup pilling, the angle is problem-first, the proof is a texture close-up, and the offer is 15% off this week.

03:38

You sell a 28-dollar body oil. You keep a problem-first hook, but it's your customer's problem: skin that feels dry again by noon.

03:48

You keep a close-up as proof, but it's your product, shot by you. You use your real offer, or no offer at all. You use your logo, your colors, your fonts.

04:02

Then put the two ads side by side. If a shopper could think they come from the same brand, you've copied too much.

04:12

And if you can't fill the proof slot with something true, change the angle. Don't invent proof.

04:25Make 3 distinct variants

04:25

Step four: make three variants that are actually different. Skip the thirty near-copies.

04:30

Meta said in December 2025 that its ads system groups ads that share key visual and thematic attributes, and treats look-alikes as variations of the same creative. That means thirty versions with a different headline color mostly count as one ad.

04:48

Make three that are actually different. Variant A keeps the winner's format with your substance. Variant B keeps the angle in a new format, say the static becomes a fifteen-second demo video. Variant C keeps the format but changes the angle or the buyer: the gift buyer instead of the self-buyer.

05:08

Three distinct concepts give the system more to work with than thirty look-alikes.

05:15

05:37

The traffic light, item by item

  • Structure, angle, format, pacing. Ideas, concepts, methods and processes sit outside US copyright.[5]
  • Wording. Short US phrases and slogans can't be registered, but the EU protected an 11-word extract.[6][7]
  • Claims. Meta prohibits deceptive or misleading practices, so back each claim with your own evidence.[11]
  • Testimonial style. Actors in a customer-style ad need a clear disclosure.[10]
  • Their footage, images and music. Protected expression; Meta rejects ads that infringe third-party copyright.[11][12]
  • Logos. Trademark law covers uses likely to confuse buyers, and so does Meta's policy.[8][12]
  • Exact copy. Protected expression in the US and, from 11 words, possibly in the EU.[5][7]
  • Real people's testimonials. Presenting their customers as yours is a fake or false testimonial under the FTC rule.[9]
  • Naming the competitor (EU). Comparative-advertising conditions apply, and confusion counts as misleading.[13][14]
